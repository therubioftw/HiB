Notes

These are the product's own setup screens, not the demo's data. I walked through every "create" screen without saving anything: Add category, Add field, Add table, Add column.

Field types are Text, Text area, Date, Number, List, Multi-select list, Currency, Employee reference, Hierarchical list and Document. Text can have a character limit or a pattern. For dates, Bob says "We cannot validate values for this type", so the field settings offer no way to require an expiry date in the future.

A category is just a name and a description. A table is a name, a description and a default permission. I saw no templates or suggestions in any of these screens.

"All permissions off" is the safe choice, and I'd keep that spirit. The problem is that access is decided later and elsewhere, so data can end up created but invisible.

Recommendation

Custom objects, set up with AI, and ready for reports and AI from day one

Where I'd go
Custom objects: records that exist on their own and link to people
  • A laptop, a certification, a country agreement. Each with its own fields and history.
  • Access, reports and Bob's AI are part of setup, not a separate step.
How admins set them up
Describe it and AI drafts it, or build it by hand
  • AI proposes the structure and checks what already exists.
  • Data-savvy admins can build directly.
  • Both end on the same review screen.

Not in the first version: custom data that payroll calculations depend on, IT asset management as a product, and org structures.

Notes

Two decisions. What gets built: custom objects, not more fields on the profile. And how: AI drafts it, a person approves it.

Why objects: a laptop is bought, assigned, returned and reassigned. A programme or an agreement involves many people. In the setup screens I saw, they can only be fields and tables on a person, and the demo's "8 places" shows what that can look like. Bob already has records like this internally: job profiles, job roles, levels and bands show up in the "Objects audit" report, and positions and projects have their own importers. I didn't find a way for customers to create their own kind.

Why AI-assisted: it's the fastest way for a non-technical admin to get a good structure, and the right moment to catch duplicates. Some enterprise admins are very data-savvy and will want full control, so the manual path stays first-class.

On scope. Allowances and agreements are in the brief, and they can feed payroll. Letting admins define data that payroll then depends on is a bigger risk than tracking certifications, so I'd leave it out of the first version and add it once objects have proven themselves. Laptops are an example, not a push into IT asset management. Org structures come later (see delivery).

Questions I'd bring to engineering first: how access works for a record nobody owns (a laptop in stock); one-to-many vs many-to-many links (a programme and its participants); history and effective dates on objects; limits and performance; and how much of the internal objects layer could be reused.

Tradeoffs

What this gives up, and where I'd land

Flexibility vs a tidy data model
Both, with guardrails. Anyone can create objects, but setup checks for similar ones and suggests reusing them.
AI speed vs admin trust
AI drafts, a person approves. Nothing is created until the admin reviews it on a normal screen.
Safe vs usable access
Inherit when it's safe, private when it isn't. The admin sees exactly who will have access before approving.
Objects now vs better tables first
Value first on today's model. Objects follow, sized with engineering. Faster if they turn out cheap.

Notes

On access. Bob already offers two options when creating a field: all permissions off, or inherit the category's permissions. It also warns that inheriting from a category with mixed permissions ends in "no access".

My rule: inherit when the category's permissions are uniform and the data isn't sensitive. Otherwise start private, for example documents, health, pay, IDs, or a category with mixed permissions, and let the admin pick who can see it from suggestions. In both cases the admin sees the resulting audience ("these 4 groups will see this") before approving.

On flexibility. The risk with any self-serve builder is recreating the "8 places" problem faster. The duplicate check is the guardrail, and archive and the audit trail that already exist stay.

On sequencing, see the delivery slide.

Notes

I don't know HiBob's architecture, team or roadmap, so there are no dates or team sizes here.

Each phase is worth shipping even if the next one slips. Phases 1 and 2 work on today's model and would help every customer who already has custom data. Phase 3 is the bigger bet.

Phase 1 is about what I saw missing: the laptop data didn't show up in the general report builder, in table import, or in the AI's answers. There are report templates named after individual tables, but I didn't open one, so I'm not claiming anything about them.

Phase 4 includes "complex organizational structures", which you mentioned for strategic deals. I see objects as the base for it, but it's its own problem space and I haven't explored it.

Notes

"Learn first" is about the riskiest assumption in the plan: that admins will trust and keep an AI-drafted structure, and that the real blocker is modelling rather than, say, services capacity. A clickable prototype with a few admins answers the first part cheaply.

"Find them" assumes HiBob can see, in aggregate, how many custom categories and fields each account has. I believe that's likely but haven't verified it.

Design partners should include both kinds of accounts: those that want the AI to draft, and data-savvy ones that build by hand.

New strategic deals are the second audience: once objects exist, they become part of the enterprise sales story.

Notes

These are proposals. I don't have baselines for any of them, and the first step would be to measure today's numbers.

The main metric is deliberately about use, because that's the gap I saw: data that exists, but that reports and the AI can't find.

Adoption and health move first, within weeks of a release. The business line moves slowly and would be read over quarters, alongside retention and expansion of the scaling accounts, and win rate in strategic deals.

The guardrail covers the two biggest risks in the plan: AI drafts that aren't good enough, and data visible to the wrong people. For access, a practical signal is how often access to a new object is changed soon after setup, or reported as wrong.
